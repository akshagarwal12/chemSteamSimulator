⬡ Octet Rule — Full Shells = Stability

Atoms with incomplete valence shells are unstable — high potential energy, like a ball on a hilltop. A full outer shell of 8 electrons creates minimum energy and maximum stability.

📋 Interaction Steps

  1. Pick a bond type from the tabs above.
  2. Watch unstable atoms pulse with incomplete shells.
  3. Click any atom to start approach via electrostatic attraction.
  4. Electrons transfer or share when atoms are close enough.
  5. Energy graph drops — system reaches stable state.
  6. Press ⚡ to add energy and return atoms to unstable state.

💡 Why Lower Energy?

Nature always seeks the path of least resistance. Electrons in incomplete shells carry excess potential energy. Filling shells is the energetic "downhill" path — driven by electrostatic force and the 2nd Law of Thermodynamics.

Real-World Applications of Electron Movement

🔋 Batteries

A lithium-ion battery exploits chemical potential energy differences between electrodes. Electrons flow from high-energy anode to low-energy cathode through a circuit — the same drive as our Na→Cl simulation, but harnessed as electricity powering your phone.

🦀 Rusting / Oxidation

Iron atoms lose electrons to oxygen in air — an ionic-like electron transfer forming iron oxides. Fe goes from unstable to more stable oxide states, releasing energy as heat. Every rusty nail is electron movement seeking lower energy, just like our simulation.

🧬 Biology — ATP & Metabolism

Cells run electron transfer chains inside mitochondria. High-energy electrons from food travel down protein chains, releasing energy at each step to produce ATP — life's energy currency. Your heartbeat is electron movement seeking stability, scaled to biology.

⚙ STEAM Integration — Technology & Engineering

This simulation uses HTML5 Canvas + JavaScript — the same foundation as professional molecular dynamics tools like AMBER and GROMACS (scaled to millions of atoms). The physics models Coulomb attraction between opposite charges; the energy graph approximates a Morse potential curve. Students building this in Scratch can use Variables for electron counts, Broadcasts for transfer events, and Clones for each electron particle — mirroring object-oriented design used by chemists and materials engineers worldwide. In PowerPoint, use motion paths and triggers to animate electrons; in Canva, build each bonding phase as a separate animated frame.
